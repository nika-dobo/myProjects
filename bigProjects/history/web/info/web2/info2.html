Telegraph of the Dead

In 1891, in a small French town called Mornier-sur-Lac, a young engineer and semi-occultist named Clémence de Voir appeared. He claimed to be able to contact the dead—not through magic, but through technology. 🧪 Experiment number 17: The Spectrogram Clémence built a machine that was a combination of a telegraph and a three-way glass prism. He said that at death, the brain emits an electrical impulse that may be stored in the air. His device would read this “stored signal.” He first tested it in his own home—the room of his mother, who had died years earlier. A few days later, the machine produced the following text: “I’m still folding your shirt under the window. Don’t forget me.” Clémence was shocked, but continued his research. 🕯️ Mornier-sur-Lac will become a landmark In 1892, a long line formed in the courtyard of his laboratory. People brought things from the dead, hoping that the telegraph would read their “last thoughts.” In some cases, the machine even made a sound — and text appeared on the cloudy glass. For example, one woman read from her deceased child: “Mother, the sun does not set there. I have borrowed your calendar.” ⚠️ Ban and disappearance The French Academy declared Clémence a charlatan. In 1894, his laboratory burned down. He himself disappeared. Only the fragments of the telegraph survived — and one note, which, as they say, he received from himself: “You are also the past now. Be the voice too.”
